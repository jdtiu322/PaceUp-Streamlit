# Training Paces

## Easy Pace

Easy pace should feel conversational. The runner should be able to speak in full sentences without gasping. Most weekly running should be easy because it builds aerobic capacity while keeping recovery costs low.

If the runner is unsure of pace, use effort first. Easy pace is usually around 3 to 4 out of 10 effort, or a pace that feels controlled enough to repeat the next day.

## Long Run Pace

Long runs are usually done at easy effort. The goal is time on feet, aerobic endurance, and durability, not proving fitness every weekend.

If a runner fades badly late in long runs, start slower, reduce the route distance, or add short fueling and hydration practice before increasing pace.

## Tempo Pace

Tempo running is comfortably hard. The runner should be able to speak short phrases, but not hold a relaxed conversation. Tempo work improves sustained speed and mental control.

For newer runners, tempo can be done as intervals such as 3 x 6 minutes at comfortably hard effort with easy jogging between reps.

## Threshold Pace

Threshold pace is around the fastest effort a runner can hold steadily for about 45 to 60 minutes when fit and rested. It should feel controlled, not like a race sprint.

Threshold workouts should end with the runner feeling like they could do a little more. If the last rep becomes a survival effort, the pace is too fast.

## Interval Pace

Interval pace is used for shorter repeats such as 400 meters, 800 meters, or 3 minute efforts. The goal is quality running mechanics and speed development, not exhaustion.

Recovery between interval reps should be long enough to keep form clean. If form falls apart, slow down or stop the workout.

## Strides

Strides are short accelerations of about 10 to 25 seconds. They should feel fast but relaxed, with smooth form and full control.

Strides are useful after easy runs, before workouts, or during a warmup. They are not all-out sprints.

## Race Pace

Race pace is specific to the target distance. A 5K pace should feel challenging early and very hard late. A marathon pace should feel controlled early and require patience.

If race pace feels impossible during training, use shorter reps at race pace with recovery instead of forcing a continuous effort too soon.

## Pace Adjustments

Heat, humidity, hills, wind, poor sleep, stress, and accumulated fatigue should all slow target paces. PaceUp should encourage runners to adjust by effort rather than forcing a watch number.

When in doubt, start easier. A workout that is slightly too easy is usually more useful than one that is too hard and disrupts the next several days.
