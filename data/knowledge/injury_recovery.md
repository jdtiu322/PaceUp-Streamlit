# Injury And Recovery

## Pain Scale

Mild discomfort that stays at 1 to 2 out of 10 and does not change running form may be monitored carefully. Pain that increases during the run, changes gait, or lingers afterward should not be ignored.

If pain reaches 4 out of 10 or higher, advise the runner to stop the session and switch to rest or low-impact cross-training.

## Stop Signs

Stop running and seek professional guidance for sharp pain, swelling, bruising, numbness, tingling, pain after a fall, suspected stress fracture, chest pain, fainting, or pain that changes stride.

PaceUp should not diagnose the condition. It should help the runner make a safer next decision.

## First Response To Pain

The first adjustment is usually to reduce load. This can mean skipping speed work, shortening the run, taking a rest day, or replacing running with cycling, swimming, walking, or another pain-free option.

Do not recommend pushing through pain to complete a planned workout. Fitness is not built by turning a small issue into a larger injury.

## Return To Running

Return to running should be gradual. Start with short easy runs or run-walk intervals. Increase only if symptoms stay calm during the session and the next day.

A simple return session can be 1 minute easy running and 1 minute walking repeated 10 times. If symptoms remain quiet, progress slowly over several sessions.

## Cross-Training

Cross-training can maintain aerobic fitness when running volume is reduced. Choose options that do not reproduce symptoms.

Effort should match the goal. Easy cross-training supports recovery, while hard cross-training can still add fatigue.

## Strength And Mobility

Strength work should support durability. Useful areas for many runners include calves, glutes, hamstrings, quads, hips, and core.

Mobility work should be gentle and targeted. Aggressive stretching into pain is not recommended.

## Recovery Basics

Sleep, food, hydration, and stress management affect recovery. If a runner keeps getting minor issues, review total training load, sleep, shoes, terrain, and recent intensity.

If symptoms persist for more than several days despite reduced training, recommend seeing a qualified clinician.
